When county leaders hear the word "data," the conversation usually defaults to IT, software updates, or servers. But data is not an IT issue; it is the fundamental currency of modern public administration.

To understand data, we must first unlearn how government traditionally stores information. Historically, counties have operated on "collections of files"—PDFs, scanned forms, locked spreadsheets, and physical paper. A file is essentially a digital piece of paper. It traps information. To answer a simple question using files, a staff member must manually read, summarize, and cross-reference multiple documents.

**Data**, by contrast, is a collection of individual, trackable units of fact. The location of a pothole, the timestamp of a 911 dispatch, the property value of a specific parcel—these are data points. When information is broken out of static files and treated as data, it can be instantly sorted, filtered, and combined.

Files force a county to look backward and react. Data allows a county to look forward, predict budget shortfalls, anticipate maintenance needs, and intelligently allocate limited resources.
